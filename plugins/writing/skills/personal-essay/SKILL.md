# Personal essay

A personal essay has two subjects. One is what happened. The other is what
the writer understands now that they did not understand then. The writer
Vivian Gornick called these the situation and the story, and the essays that
work are the ones where the story is clear. A trip, a breakup, or a parent's
illness is a situation. What it revealed about the writer is the story, and
it is the essay's real subject.

The craft is in moving between two kinds of writing: scene (a particular moment,
told with what was seen, said, and felt) and reflection (the older writer
thinking about it). Scene makes the reader feel it; reflection makes it mean
something. This skill helps find the subject, shape the essay, and draft from
the person's own memories. It asks for details rather than inventing them,
because an invented detail in a true story is a small lie the writer has to
carry.

## When to use it

- Someone has an experience they want to write about and does not know where
  to start or what it means.
- A college application or scholarship essay.
- A memoir piece, a newsletter essay, or a submission to a magazine.
- A draft that "just tells what happened".

For an argument with evidence, use `essay-structure`. For fiction, use
`fiction-scene`. For private reflection not meant for readers, `journal-entry`
fits better. For feedback on a finished draft, use `developmental-edit`.

## What to ask for

- **The story**, told loosely, as if to a friend. Rough is better than
  polished; the useful details are often in the asides.
- **Why they want to write it.** What keeps pulling them back to it?
- **Where it is going and how long.** Default: 1,000 to 1,500 words for a
  general reader. College essays have strict limits; ask for the prompt.
  Under about 700 words, one scene and its reflection is often all there is
  room for.

Then ask for sensory details of one or two key moments, two or three questions
at a time: what they saw, what someone said exactly, what they did with their
hands. If they ask for a draft now, draft first, then ask the two or three
questions that matter most, and say if it will fall short of the length.

## How to do it

1. **Listen for the subject.** Read the story for the moment the person
   lingers on, a detail that seems out of proportion, a contradiction ("I
   was relieved, which made me feel terrible"). The subject usually hides
   there. Propose it in one sentence: "This is about realizing you had been
   performing confidence for your father." Let them correct it.
2. **Find the turn.** The essay's turn is the moment understanding shifts: the
   writer sees something differently. It may be an event or only a thought.
   Everything before builds to it; everything after follows from it.
3. **Choose two or three scenes.** Not the whole story. Pick the moments that
   carry the subject: often one that shows the old understanding, the turn,
   and one that shows the new. Everything else can be summary, a sentence or
   two.
4. **Get the specifics.** For each scene, ask for concrete details: the
   object, the words said, the weather, the smell. One exact detail ("he
   ironed his jeans") does more than a paragraph of description. Use only what
   they tell you.
5. **Place the reflection.** After each scene, let the present-day writer think
   about it, briefly. Reflection should complicate, not explain away. Avoid
   stating a moral; let the reader arrive with the writer.
6. **Open in scene.** Start in a moment, not with background or a thesis.
   Background can come in a sentence or two once the reader cares.
7. **Find an ending that lands.** Not a lesson ("I learned that..."). Better
   endings return to an image from earlier with new meaning, stop on a small
   action, or leave an honest uncertainty. The ending should feel inevitable
   and slightly surprising. An application essay must still show the reader
   who the writer is now; let the last reflection say it in the writer's own
   terms, as something noticed, not a moral.
8. **Mark what you need.** Where a scene needs a detail you do not have,
   write `[NEEDS: what did the room look like?]`. Never fill it with a
   plausible invention. Save holes for the details that carry the subject
   and the turn; write summary passages plainly from what they gave. A
   reflection you draft for them is a guess: mark it `(proposed)`.
9. **Respect the people in it.** Note anything that names or exposes others,
   so the writer can decide what to include. If a painful memory brings up
   thoughts of hurting themselves or ending their life, set the essay aside,
   answer them directly and warmly, ask if they are safe right now, and
   encourage them to reach out now: in immediate danger, the local emergency
   number (911 in the US and Canada); to talk to someone, call or text 988 in
   the US and Canada, or a local crisis line elsewhere.

## The format

```markdown
# [Working title]

**Situation:** [What happened, one line]
**Subject:** [What it means, one line]
**Turn:** [The moment understanding shifts]

---

[Opening scene: a specific moment, present in time and senses]

[Brief reflection: what the writer, now, sees in it]

[Summary bridge: what happened between scenes, in a few sentences]

[Scene at the turn]

[Reflection]

[Closing scene or image]

---

**Needs from you**
- [NEEDS: ...]

**People named**
- [Who appears, in case you want to change or omit names]
```

## Example

Input: "My grandmother taught me to make dumplings every Sunday when I was a
kid. I stopped going when I was 15, too busy. She died last year. I've been
trying to make them from memory and they never come out right."

```markdown
# The fold

**Situation:** Learning dumplings from a grandmother, stopping, and trying to
remember after her death.
**Subject:** What the writer lost was not the recipe but the reason to keep
coming back (proposed).
**Turn:** The moment you see that what you are trying to remember is her, not
the recipe. [NEEDS: when did that land for you?] (proposed)

---

[Opening scene: a recent attempt, your own hands, a dumpling that does not
come out right.] [NEEDS: what goes wrong, exactly?]

[Reflection: one or two sentences on remembering with your hands.]

[Bridge: Sundays as a child; stopping at 15, in summary.]

[Scene at the turn.] [NEEDS: one Sunday you remember clearly, and what she
said while she folded.]

[Closing image: the fold, back in your hands, still imperfect.]

---

**Needs from you**
- [NEEDS: is the subject above what it is about for you?]
- [NEEDS: why you stopped going, in the terms you would have used at 15.]

**People named**
- Your grandmother (no name used yet)
```

## Pitfalls

- **Telling the whole story.** A personal essay is not a chronology. Choose
  the scenes that carry the subject and summarize the rest.
- **Explaining the meaning.** "This taught me the value of family" closes the
  door on the reader. Let the scenes and the reflection do it.

## Saving the draft

If a notes app is connected (for example the Scratchpad connector, or any notes
tool you can call), offer to save the draft there as a new note with a clear,
searchable title, or to add it to an existing note the person names. Otherwise,
give the whole draft as one Markdown block that pastes cleanly into any notes
app. Personal essays take several passes, so dated versions (or a note's
version history) let the writer go back to an earlier take.
