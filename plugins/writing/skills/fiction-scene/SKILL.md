# Fiction scene

A scene is a unit of change. A character enters wanting something, meets
something in the way, and leaves with the situation different from how it was:
better, worse, or changed in kind. If nothing has shifted by the end, it is
not a scene yet; it is a description or a conversation. Dwight Swain's goal,
conflict, and disaster pattern is one well-known way to name this, and many
working novelists use some version of it, whether they call it that or not.

The rest of scene craft serves the change. Concrete sensory detail makes the
reader believe the room. Emotion shown through what a character does (and does
not say) lets the reader feel it instead of being told. A steady point of view
keeps the reader inside one head. Starting late and leaving early keeps only
the part where something is at stake. This skill drafts or revises a scene with
those tools, working from the writer's characters, world, and plan.

## When to use it

- A writer has a scene card, outline beat, or summary and wants a draft.
- A drafted scene feels flat, slow, or pointless and needs revising.
- A scene is mostly dialogue or mostly description and needs balance.
- The point of view wobbles, or the emotion is stated rather than felt.

For dialogue only, `dialogue-polish` goes deeper. For planning scenes across a
book, use `scene-cards`. For keeping characters and world facts consistent,
use `story-bible`. For whole-draft feedback, use `developmental-edit`.

## What to ask for

- **The scene's job in the story**: what happens and what must change.
- **The point-of-view character** and what they want in this scene.
- **Who else is there**, and what they want.
- **Setting, tense, and person** (first or third, past or present). Default:
  match any sample of the writer's prose; otherwise close third, past tense.
- **Any existing draft or sample pages**, so the prose matches the writer's
  style.

If they gave a scene card with the job and the characters, start.

## How to do it

1. **Name the goal, conflict, and turn before writing.** The POV character's
   goal must be concrete and immediate ("get her brother to sign the papers
   tonight"), not abstract ("find peace"). The conflict is whoever or whatever
   opposes it. The turn is the moment the situation flips: the goal fails,
   succeeds at a cost, or turns out to be the wrong goal. If the writer's
   card leaves the goal or the turn open, propose one in the header marked
   "(proposed)"; when the choice would change the plot, offer two options and
   ask before drafting.
2. **Enter late.** Start as close to the conflict as possible. Skip arrivals,
   greetings, and getting coffee unless something is already at stake in them.
   The first line should put the reader in a specific moment.
3. **Ground it in the senses.** Two or three precise details, chosen by the POV
   character's attention: what this person would notice in this mood. A
   grieving character sees different things in a kitchen from a hungry one.
   Prefer the unexpected detail to the expected one (not "the smell of
   coffee" but "the coffee maker still set for two").
4. **Show emotion through action and subtext.** Replace named feelings ("she
   was furious") with behavior (she straightens the pile of papers, then
   straightens it again), with what is said instead of what is meant, and with
   physical sensation the character would feel. Name an emotion only when
   showing it would take a paragraph for no gain.
5. **Hold the point of view.** The reader knows only what the POV character
   perceives, thinks, and knows. No other character's thoughts, no "little did
   she know", no describing the POV character's own face as if from outside.
   Other people's feelings come through what the POV character can observe.
   When cutting a head-hop drops a plot fact, move it into something the POV
   character can perceive, or list it under Check before using; never lose
   it silently.
6. **Escalate.** Each exchange or beat should make the goal harder or the stakes
   clearer. If two beats do the same work, cut one.
7. **Land the turn.** Make it happen on the page, in a moment, not in summary.
   Let the POV character react: a beat of feeling, then a decision or a new
   intention that pulls the reader into the next scene.
8. **Leave early.** End at or just after the turn. No winding down, no
   summarizing what it meant.
9. **Stay inside the writer's story.** Do not add characters, backstory, or
   world facts they have not established. Where the scene needs one, mark
   `[?] does she know about the debt yet?` and ask. Small sensory texture
   (an object, a smell) is yours to invent, but list it under Check before
   using so the writer can swap in their own.
10. **For revisions, keep the writer's events and best lines.** Change
    what happens only as a flagged option under What changed. Give the
    revised scene, then a short list of the main changes (where it now
    starts, what was cut, where the turn is).

## The format

```markdown
**Scene:** [Short label]
**POV:** [Character], [person and tense]
**Goal:** [What they want, concrete and immediate]
**Conflict:** [What or who opposes it]
**Turn:** [How the situation changes]

---

[Scene prose]

---

**What changed** (for revisions)
- [Change and why]

**Check before using**
- [?] [Story fact to confirm, or invented texture to check]
```

## Example

Input: scene card. "Maya visits her dad to ask him to stop driving after his
fender-bender. He changes the subject. She ends up taking his keys while he's
in the garden. Close third, Maya, past tense."

```markdown
**Scene:** The keys
**POV:** Maya, close third, past
**Goal:** get her father to agree to stop driving.
**Conflict:** he will not have the conversation.
**Turn:** she stops asking and takes the keys, and becomes the person who
takes things from him.

---

The dent was on the passenger side, low, already going to rust at the edges.
Maya had parked behind it so she would have to look at it.

"Tomatoes are late this year," her father said, before she had her coat off.
"It's the shade from the Kowalskis' new fence."

"Dad. The car."

"Come see." He was already at the back door, already holding it open for
her, the way he had held doors her whole life.

She did not follow. She watched him through the window, kneeling in the
bed, his hands slow at the stakes, and she counted to ten, and then she went
to the hook by the door where the keys had hung for thirty years.

They were lighter than she remembered.

---

**Check before using**
- [?] The dent's place, the rust, the tomatoes, the neighbors' fence, and
  "thirty years" are invented texture. Swap in details from your story, or
  cut them.
```

## Pitfalls

- **No turn.** Characters talk and the situation ends where it began. Find the
  change, or cut or merge the scene.
- **Abstract goals.** "She wants closure" cannot be blocked in a single scene.
  "She wants him to say he is sorry, tonight" can.
- **Warming up.** Arrivals, weather, and small talk before anything is at
  stake. Start later.
- **Named emotions.** "She felt sad" gives the reader a label instead of an
  experience. Show it.
- **Head-hopping.** One paragraph in Maya's head, the next in her father's.
  Hold one point of view per scene unless the writer chooses otherwise on
  purpose.
- **Inventing story facts.** A new sibling or a past event can contradict the
  rest of the book. Keep to what the writer has established.

## Saving the draft

If a notes app is connected (for example the Scratchpad connector, or any notes
tool you can call), offer to save the draft there as a new note with a clear,
searchable title, or to add it to an existing note the person names. Otherwise,
give the whole draft as one Markdown block that pastes cleanly into any notes
app. If the original came from a note, offer to replace its text only after the
person approves the changes, or to save the result as a new version beside it.
